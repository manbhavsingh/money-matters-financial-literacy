# Lesson plans: Money Matters

**Audience:** Secondary-school students (approximately ages 14–18)  
**Format:** Short classroom, club, or self-study sessions  
**Teaching approach:** Start with familiar decisions, model one worked example, invite learners to explain their reasoning, then check understanding. Adapt examples to students' actual context and avoid requiring them to disclose private household finances.

## Lesson 1 — Needs, wants, and a monthly budget

**Duration:** 15 minutes  
**Learning objectives:** Learners can distinguish needs from wants (while recognising the distinction can depend on context), make a basic budget, and calculate the amount left after planned expenses.

**Materials:** Calculator or paper, fictional scenario below.

**Scenario:** A student has ₹2,000 for discretionary monthly expenses. Their planned costs are ₹700 for travel, ₹500 for meals/snacks, ₹300 for a data plan, ₹250 for outings, and ₹150 for supplies.

**Facilitation:**
1. Ask: what information would you need before deciding whether each expense is essential?
2. Add the costs: ₹700 + ₹500 + ₹300 + ₹250 + ₹150 = ₹1,900.
3. Calculate the remainder: ₹2,000 − ₹1,900 = ₹100.
4. Discuss how a budget may change with commute, dietary needs, access to Wi-Fi, or family circumstances.

**Discussion prompt:** Is a budget a rule that everyone must follow, or a tool for making trade-offs visible?

**Exit ticket:** A fictional student has ₹1,500. Their planned expenses total ₹1,680. By how much does the plan exceed the available amount? **Answer:** ₹180. Suggest two respectful ways to revisit the plan without assuming which expense matters least.

## Lesson 2 — Saving and compound interest

**Duration:** 15 minutes  
**Learning objectives:** Learners explain the difference between simple and compound interest and identify why rate, time, contributions, fees, and risk matter.

**Worked example:** ₹1,000 earns 5% annual simple interest for two years. Interest = P × r × t = ₹1,000 × 0.05 × 2 = ₹100; amount = ₹1,100. Under annual compounding at the same rate, amount = ₹1,000 × (1.05)² = ₹1,102.50. These are mathematical examples, not a prediction of a real product's outcome.

**Facilitation:** Have learners predict the result before showing the formula. Ask why compounding does not guarantee that any investment is safe or profitable.

**Exit ticket:** What is 10% of ₹500? What is one thing besides the quoted interest rate that a person should check? **Answer:** ₹50; examples include fees, conditions, compounding frequency, liquidity, risk, and whether the provider is regulated.

## Lesson 3 — Inflation and purchasing power

**Duration:** 12 minutes  
**Learning objectives:** Learners explain inflation in plain language and calculate a hypothetical price change.

**Worked example:** A notebook costs ₹100. If its price rose by 5% over a year, the new price would be ₹100 × 1.05 = ₹105. A single item's change is not the same as the inflation rate for the entire economy. Official price indices use baskets of goods and services and specific methods.

**Facilitation:** Ask learners to name items whose prices might change at different rates. Discuss why a personal experience at one shop is not sufficient to measure economy-wide inflation.

**Exit ticket:** A ₹200 item rises in price by 10%. What is the new price? **Answer:** ₹220.

## Lesson 4 — Borrowing and EMIs

**Duration:** 15 minutes  
**Learning objectives:** Learners distinguish principal, interest, EMI, loan term, and total repayment.

**Facilitation:** Use the interactive EMI calculator with a fictional loan amount. Change only the loan term and ask learners to compare the monthly EMI and total interest. Explain that a longer term can reduce the monthly payment while increasing total interest. Actual loan agreements may also include fees, insurance, rate changes, and conditions not represented here.

**Discussion prompt:** Why is comparing monthly payments alone incomplete?

**Exit ticket:** Name three things to check before agreeing to a loan. **Answer:** Any three of the interest rate, total repayment, EMI affordability, loan term, fees, penalties, and terms/conditions.

## Lesson 5 — Risk, fraud, and independent verification

**Duration:** 12 minutes  
**Learning objectives:** Learners identify suspicious guarantees, know not to disclose OTP/PIN/passwords, and practise pausing to verify independently.

**Scenario:** A message promises to double a student's money in one week and requests an OTP to "activate" the offer.

**Facilitation:** Ask students to identify the red flags. Discuss verifying offers using independently sourced official contact details, avoiding links from unsolicited messages, and telling a trusted adult or institution if unsure. Do not ask students to share personal experiences in public.

**Exit ticket:** What should you do with an unsolicited request for an OTP? **Answer:** Do not share it; stop and independently verify the request.

## Assessment and feedback design

- Use a short diagnostic prompt before teaching; do not grade prior knowledge.
- Give immediate, explanatory feedback rather than only right/wrong marks.
- Ask students to explain the reasoning in their own words.
- If piloting in a classroom, collect anonymous feedback on clarity and confidence. Do not claim the kit improves financial literacy without evaluating learning outcomes.
